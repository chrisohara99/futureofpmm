Unit 11

The PMM AI Tech Stack

Opening Scenario: The Tool Sprawl Problem

Marcus, a PMM director at a mid-size B2B software company, opened his team's budget spreadsheet and counted: fourteen AI tools. His five-person team had accumulated subscriptions to three different content platforms, two competitive intelligence systems, a transcription service, an image generator, two general-purpose LLMs, and a handful of point solutions he'd honestly forgotten existed.

The irony wasn't lost on him. He'd approved each tool individually because each solved a real problem. But somewhere between "this will save us hours" and today, his team was spending more time switching between tools—re-entering context, reformatting outputs, reconciling contradictory information—than the tools were saving. The cognitive load of managing the stack had become its own burden.

Over the following quarter, Marcus rebuilt his team's approach from the ground up. Not by eliminating AI tools—they were too valuable for that—but by establishing principles for what belonged in the stack and what didn't. By Q2, his team was down to four tools, producing better work faster, and spending their time on strategy instead of tool management.

The question this unit explores: How do you build an AI tech stack that amplifies your effectiveness rather than fragmenting your attention?

The Three-Layer Stack Architecture

The most effective PMM tech stacks share a common structure: three distinct layers, each serving a different purpose in the workflow.

Layer 1: The Foundation (General-Purpose LLM)

Every PMM needs one primary general-purpose LLM as their Swiss Army knife. This is your default tool for:

  • First-draft generation for any content type
  • Research synthesis and summarization
  • Brainstorming and ideation
  • Data analysis and pattern recognition
  • Copy editing and refinement

The key word is one. Having multiple general-purpose LLMs creates decision fatigue ("Which should I use for this?") without proportional benefit. Pick one, learn it deeply, and build your workflows around it.

Layer 2: The Research Engine

General-purpose LLMs have a knowledge cutoff and can't access real-time information. Your research layer fills that gap:

  • Web-grounded search with citations (Perplexity, Google AI Overview)
  • Competitive monitoring feeds
  • Industry news aggregation

This layer answers the question: "What's happening now that the LLM doesn't know about?"

Layer 3: Specialist Tools (Selective)

The third layer is where most teams make mistakes—adding tools because they're interesting rather than necessary. A specialist tool earns its place only when:

  • It does something your foundation layer genuinely can't do
  • You'll use it at least weekly
  • The workflow integration is clean (not another copy-paste step)

The Integration Test

Before adding any specialist tool, ask: "Does this integrate into my existing workflow, or does it create a new workflow?" Tools that require you to context-switch, re-enter information, or maintain a separate mental model are usually not worth their cognitive cost.

Tool Categories Worth Evaluating

Not every tool category is equally relevant for PMM work. Here's an honest assessment:

Category When It's Worth It When to Skip
Competitive Intelligence Platforms (Klue, Crayon) 5+ direct competitors, complex landscape, dedicated CI motion 2-3 competitors, simple landscape, can monitor manually
Content Generation (Jasper, Writer) High-volume commodity content (emails, social, ads) Strategic content, thought leadership, anything voice-sensitive
Transcription/Notes (Otter, Fireflies) Frequent customer calls, sales shadowing, research interviews Occasional meetings, existing tools cover it
Image Generation (Midjourney, DALL-E) Rapid concept visualization, internal presentations Customer-facing assets (brand risk), have design resources
Demo Platforms (Consensus, Demostack) PLG motion, self-serve evaluation, high demo volume Enterprise sales-led, custom demos required

The Build vs. Buy Decision

For technically inclined PMMs, there's always the temptation to build custom solutions—a CI monitoring pipeline, a content workflow, a knowledge management system. The appeal is real: custom tools do exactly what you need.

The hidden cost is maintenance. Here's the honest trade-off:

Buy When:

  • The workflow is stable and well-defined
  • You need reliability (production systems, team-wide tools)
  • The vendor handles updates, integrations, and edge cases
  • Your time is better spent on strategy than infrastructure

Build When:

  • The workflow is experimental or unique to your organization
  • Existing tools don't fit your specific process
  • You have the technical skills and time to maintain it
  • The build is a learning investment, not just a tool

The Maintenance Trap

Custom tools work beautifully until they don't. RSS feeds break. APIs change. The person who built it leaves. Before building anything custom, ask: "Who maintains this when I'm on vacation, promoted, or gone?" If the answer is unclear, buy instead.

Workshop Exercise: The Stack Audit

List every AI tool you currently use or have access to. For each one, answer:

  1. Frequency: When did you last use it? (Today / This week / This month / Can't remember)
  2. Overlap: Does another tool do the same thing?
  3. Integration: Does it fit your workflow or require context-switching?
  4. Value: What would you lose if you cancelled it tomorrow?

Deliverable: A "keep / consolidate / cancel" decision for each tool, with your reasoning.

The Enterprise Reality: Security and Governance

At SAP and other large enterprises, you can't just subscribe to any tool that looks useful. IT and security have legitimate concerns about data handling, and navigating them is part of the job.

The Three Questions Security Will Ask:

  1. Data residency: Where does your data go? Is it stored in approved regions?
  2. Training policy: Does the vendor use your data to train their models?
  3. Access controls: Can unauthorized users access your competitive intelligence or pricing data?

Pro Tip: Lead the Conversation

Don't wait for security to ask. Come to them with answers prepared:

  • "Here's the tool, here's the enterprise tier with SOC 2 compliance"
  • "Here's the vendor's data processing agreement"
  • "Here's what data we'll put in it—nothing customer-specific, no pricing"

PMMs who frame AI adoption as a business case with risk mitigation get approved faster than those who ask for permission to experiment.

The Recommended Stack by Team Size

Solo PMM or Team of 2-3

  • Foundation: One enterprise LLM (Claude or ChatGPT Team)
  • Research: Perplexity Pro
  • That's it. Seriously. The general-purpose LLM covers 90% of what a small team needs. Add specialist tools only when you hit a specific, recurring pain point.

Team of 5-15

  • Foundation: Enterprise LLM with team workspace
  • Research: Web-grounded search
  • Plus 1-2 specialists: CI platform if competitive landscape warrants it, transcription if customer call volume is high

Team of 15+

  • At this scale, you need a designated owner for the stack
  • The risk is fragmentation—different people using different tools, inconsistent processes
  • Invest in shared knowledge bases, common taxonomies, and unified workflows

Key Principles (Summary)

  1. Fewer tools, used deeply. One tool you've mastered beats five tools you dabble with.
  2. Foundation first. Get excellent at your primary LLM before adding specialists.
  3. Integration over features. A tool that fits your workflow beats a tool with more capabilities that doesn't.
  4. Maintenance matters. Every tool you add is a tool you have to maintain, update, and remember to use.
  5. Security proactively. Frame AI adoption as a business case with risk mitigation, not a request for permission.
