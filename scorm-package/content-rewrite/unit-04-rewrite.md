## Opening Scenario: Two Conversations, Same Week

**Monday:** A call with Gartner. The analyst is preparing the next Magic Quadrant for our category. She has detailed questions about our roadmap, customer references in manufacturing, and how we handle data sovereignty in EMEA. We've prepared extensively. The call goes well.

**Thursday:** A prospect's procurement team asks Claude to evaluate vendors in our category. Claude synthesizes analyst reports, G2 reviews, technical documentation, and Reddit threads. It produces a shortlist recommendation. We're not on it.

Same week. Same company. Same product. One conversation with a human evaluator who knows us. One conversation with an AI evaluator who doesn't.

The skills for winning both are more similar than you'd think.

---

## The Evaluator Parallel

Human analysts and AI assistants are both *systematic evaluators*. They:
- Process large amounts of information
- Apply consistent frameworks
- Look for verifiable evidence
- Weight credible third-party sources
- Penalize inconsistency and gaps
- Reward clarity and structure

The difference isn't what they value—it's *how* they process information.

| Human Analysts | AI Assistants |
|---------------|---------------|
| Deep research over weeks/months | Synthesize in seconds |
| Relationship context matters | No relationship memory |
| Can ask clarifying questions | Work with what's available |
| Weight narrative and vision | Weight explicit statements |
| Influenced by briefings | Can't be briefed |
| Update reports annually | Real-time synthesis |

**The insight:** PMMs who are good at analyst relations already have most of the skills for AI visibility. They just need to apply them differently.

## The Evidence Hierarchy

Both analysts and AI assistants evaluate claims against evidence. Not all evidence is equal.

### Tier 1: Verified Third-Party Validation
*Highest credibility*

- Published analyst research (Gartner MQ, Forrester Wave)
- Customer case studies with named companies and metrics
- G2/Peer Insights reviews (volume and recency matter)
- Awards with transparent criteria
- Independent benchmarks and tests

### Tier 2: Credible Internal Claims
*Moderate credibility*

- Technical documentation with specific capabilities
- Product demonstrations (for human evaluators)
- Customer quotes (named > anonymous)
- Roadmap commitments (with track record)

### Tier 3: Marketing Assertions
*Low credibility*

- Superlatives ("best-in-class," "leading," "revolutionary")
- Claims without evidence
- Positioning statements without differentiation
- Vision without proof points

**The pattern:** Both analysts and AI assistants discount Tier 3 and prioritize Tier 1. The question is whether your most important claims have Tier 1 evidence.

## Analyst Relations in Practice

### The Annual Cycle

**Major research:** Magic Quadrants, Waves, MarketScapes
- Timeline: Usually 6-9 months from start to publication
- Input opportunities: Initial briefing, inquiry calls, customer references, written responses

**Ongoing research:** Market Guides, trend reports, advisory
- Timeline: Continuous
- Input opportunities: Regular briefings, inquiry usage, event presence

### What Analysts Actually Value

**1. Clarity over cleverness**
Analysts evaluate dozens of vendors. Clear, consistent positioning wins over creative messaging that requires interpretation.

**2. Evidence over assertions**
Every claim should have a proof point. Customer references, usage metrics, technical validation.

**3. Roadmap credibility**
Not just what you're building—your track record of delivering what you promised.

**4. Customer perspective**
Analysts want to hear from customers directly. Reference availability matters.

**5. Intellectual honesty**
Acknowledging weaknesses builds credibility. Every product has gaps. Analysts know this.

### The Briefing Formula

**Opening (2 min):** Context on what's changed since last briefing
**Product update (10 min):** Key capabilities, differentiation, evidence
**Customer proof (5 min):** Specific examples with metrics
**Roadmap (5 min):** Near-term commitments, longer-term direction
**Market perspective (5 min):** Your view on trends, competition
**Q&A (15 min):** Their questions, not your agenda

**The trap:** Treating briefings as sales pitches. Analysts aren't buying—they're evaluating.

## AI Agents as Evaluators

AI assistants can't be briefed. They synthesize what's publicly available. But the same principles apply.

### What AI Assistants Look For

**1. Clear capability statements**
AI extracts explicit claims: "SAP Datasphere does X." Not: "In today's complex landscape, modern data architectures require..."

**2. Verifiable differentiation**
AI builds competitive maps. It needs to understand what makes you different. Vague uniqueness claims don't parse.

**3. Third-party validation**
AI weights G2 reviews, analyst mentions, and customer proof. Marketing claims without validation get discounted.

**4. Consistency across sources**
AI synthesizes from multiple sources. Contradictions create uncertainty. Consistent messaging everywhere matters.

**5. Recent information**
AI training has cutoff dates, but retrieval-augmented systems pull recent content. Current documentation beats outdated.

### The GEO Checklist (from Unit 2)

- [ ] Lead with explicit capability statements
- [ ] Use buyer terminology, not internal jargon
- [ ] Make differentiation clear and specific
- [ ] Ensure third-party sources support your positioning
- [ ] Remove contradictions across properties
- [ ] Keep documentation current

## Workshop Exercise 2: The Dual Audience Test

Take your core positioning statement or boilerplate.

**Test 1: Analyst Perspective**
If you were a Gartner analyst evaluating this, what questions would you have? What evidence would you want?

**Test 2: AI Perspective**
If an AI assistant had to summarize this in two sentences, what would it say? Is that accurate? Is it differentiated?

**Compare:** Where do the two perspectives align? Where do they diverge?

## The Review Imperative

G2 and Gartner Peer Insights matter more than ever. Both analysts and AI assistants treat them as ground truth.

### The Math That Matters

- **Volume:** More reviews = more signal. Under 50 reviews in a category = visibility risk.
- **Recency:** Reviews older than 18 months get less weight. Continuous collection matters.
- **Sentiment:** Overall rating matters, but so does sentiment on specific criteria.
- **Response:** How vendors respond to negative reviews signals maturity.

### Building a Review Engine

**1. Identify happy customers**
Use NPS data, support satisfaction, and CSM input.

**2. Make asking easy**
Timing (after positive moments), simplicity (one-click links), and motivation (not incentives—they backfire).

**3. Systematize**
This isn't a one-time push. Build review requests into customer lifecycle.

**4. Monitor and respond**
Negative reviews are inevitable. Professional responses matter.

---

## Key Principles (Summary)

1. **Analysts and AI are parallel evaluators.** Same values (evidence, clarity, consistency), different processing.

2. **Evidence has tiers.** Third-party validation > credible internal claims > marketing assertions.

3. **Briefings aren't pitches.** Analysts evaluate; they don't buy. Intellectual honesty builds credibility.

4. **AI can't be briefed.** Visibility depends entirely on what's publicly available and parseable.

5. **Reviews are ground truth.** Both analysts and AI weight G2/Peer Insights heavily. Build a systematic review engine.
